Why Arguments in Relationships Rarely Feel Like What They're About

Mitko · February 18, 2026 · 6 min read

The dishes aren't about the dishes. The forgotten errand isn't about the errand. When couples come to see me, the thing they're arguing about is almost never the thing they're actually arguing about.

In over a decade of working with couples, one pattern repeats with remarkable consistency: the surface-level conflict — who forgot to take out the bins, why one person is "always on their phone," whether the holiday plans were agreed or assumed — is rarely the real source of pain. Beneath the words, something deeper is speaking. And until we learn to hear it, the same arguments will keep cycling back.

The Music Beneath the Words

Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT), the approach I use in my practice, describes couples as engaged in a kind of emotional dance. Each person has moves — ways of reaching out, ways of pulling back — and over time, these moves become choreographed into patterns that repeat, often without either person choosing them consciously.

When one partner criticises (often what happens when someone feels ignored and doesn't know how to say so), the other may withdraw (often what happens when someone feels overwhelmed and doesn't know how to name that). The criticising triggers the withdrawal. The withdrawal triggers more criticising. Neither person is the villain. Both are scared.

Beneath most fights is a person saying: "Are you there for me? Do I matter to you? Am I safe with you?"

These are the fundamental questions of attachment. They're the same questions we had as children — and they don't disappear when we grow up and enter romantic relationships. They just get rerouted through arguments about the dishwasher.

What We Do Instead of Saying What We Need

Here's the painful irony: most people who initiate arguments aren't trying to fight. They're trying to connect. Criticism, nagging, and "going on about things" are often bids for closeness that have been deformed by the fear that a more direct, vulnerable bid will be rejected.

Because saying "I feel lonely and I'm scared you don't need me as much as I need you" is terrifying. It requires exposing something raw and real. It risks being dismissed. So instead, we say: "You never listen to me."

And the person on the receiving end? They may feel attacked, so they defend, or go quiet, or walk out of the room — not because they don't care, but because they feel overwhelmed and don't know what else to do. From the outside, this looks like not caring. From the inside, it's self-protection.

The pursue-withdraw pattern

This is one of the most common dynamics I see: one partner pursues — escalating in tone or frequency — and the other withdraws. Both are in pain. Both are trying to protect themselves and, paradoxically, protect the relationship. Neither feels heard. Both feel increasingly alone.

Until someone names the pattern — and the vulnerability beneath it — the dance continues. Often for years.

What Actually Helps

The good news is that once couples begin to recognise their own dance, things can shift quite rapidly. Seeing the pattern — even naming it gently in the middle of a conflict ("I think we're doing our thing again") — creates a small but significant moment of stepping outside it.

What helps even more is learning to speak the language underneath the argument. Not "you're always distracted" but "I miss you. I want to feel like we're on the same team." Not silence and withdrawal, but "I'm feeling overwhelmed right now and I need a few minutes, but I do want to come back to this."

These aren't easy sentences to say. They require a kind of vulnerability that feels enormously risky, especially if your history — in this relationship or earlier ones — has taught you that vulnerability isn't safe. But they are what breaks cycles.

A Gentle Invitation

If you and your partner find yourselves having the same argument again and again, try pausing and asking: What is this really about for me? Not "why is my partner wrong" — but what am I feeling underneath the frustration? What am I scared of? What do I need that I haven't been able to say out loud?

You don't have to solve it alone. That's what therapy is for — a space where both people can slow down, be heard, and start to hear each other differently.

The path back toward each other is usually shorter than couples fear. It just begins somewhere more vulnerable than an argument about dishes.
